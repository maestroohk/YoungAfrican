AI assistants are now part of ordinary work: drafting emails, summarising reports, tidying spreadsheets, explaining unfamiliar terms. Used well, they free up time for the parts of a job that need judgement. Used carelessly, they can quietly take over that judgement too.

This guide is for students, office staff, small business owners and community leaders who already use tools such as ChatGPT, Copilot or Gemini, or are about to. It sets out what the research says, then five habits, each with a short workplace scenario. **The scenarios are illustrations we have written to show the habits in practice; they are not real cases.**

## What the research actually says

The most direct study so far comes from Carnegie Mellon University and Microsoft Research. [Lee and colleagues (CHI 2025)](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-impact-of-generative-ai-on-critical-thinking-self-reported-reductions-in-cognitive-effort-and-confidence-effects-from-a-survey-of-knowledge-workers/) surveyed 319 knowledge workers, who described 936 real examples of using generative AI at work. Three findings are worth knowing:

- **Confidence cuts both ways.** Higher confidence in the AI was associated with less critical thinking. Higher confidence in one's own ability to do the task was associated with more.
- **The work of thinking shifts.** Rather than gathering information or solving problems from scratch, people spent their effort on verifying AI output, fitting it into their work and overseeing the task, what the authors call "task stewardship".
- **The barriers are ordinary.** Participants skipped critical thinking when they lacked time, when a task felt trivial, when they trusted the tool, or when they did not know enough about the topic to judge whether the answer was right.

The study has limits the authors acknowledge: it relies on self-reports, it was conducted only in English, and its sample leaned towards younger, more tech-savvy workers. It shows associations, not proof that AI makes people think less. But it matches older evidence.

Long before chatbots, researchers studied **automation bias**, which a [systematic review by Goddard, Roudsari and Wyatt](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3240751) defines as "the tendency to over-rely on automation". Reviewing 74 studies of decision support systems, they found it was made worse by heavy workloads, complex tasks and time pressure, and by high trust in the system. Training and making users clearly accountable for decisions helped reduce it. In four pooled healthcare studies, wrong advice from a clinical decision support system increased the risk of an incorrect decision by 26%.

The lesson is not "avoid AI". It is that the conditions of a busy workplace, such as deadlines, routine tasks and a tool that usually seems right, are exactly the conditions in which people stop checking.

## Habit 1: Verify anything that matters

AI assistants produce fluent text whether or not it is accurate. The UK's [National Cyber Security Centre](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/blog-post/chatgpt-and-large-language-models-whats-the-risk) warned in 2023 that large language models "can get things wrong and 'hallucinate' incorrect facts", and that they can give convincing-sounding answers that are only partly correct.

*Scenario (illustration):* Aisha, a finance assistant at a Kampala NGO, asks an AI tool to summarise a donor's reporting requirements from a long PDF. The summary is neat and confident. Before sending it to her manager, she checks three things against the original: the deadline, the currency of the budget template and the list of required attachments. One date is wrong.

**In practice:**

- Decide in advance which details must be checked: names, figures, dates, legal or policy statements, anything that will be sent outside your team.
- Check against the original document or an authoritative source, not by asking the same AI whether it is sure.
- The higher the stakes, the more you check. A rough brainstorm needs little; a payment instruction needs a lot.

## Habit 2: The first draft is mine

The Lee study draws on an older idea from automation research: when machines handle routine work, people lose the practice that keeps their judgement sharp, and are less ready when something unusual happens. Writing, analysing and planning are skills that weaken without use.

*Scenario (illustration):* David, a recent graduate at a logistics firm in Jinja, has to write a short proposal for a new delivery route. He writes a rough one-page outline himself first, including his own reasoning on costs and risks, and only then asks an AI assistant to suggest gaps and tighten the wording. When his supervisor asks why he chose the route, he can explain it, because the argument is his.

**In practice:**

- For work that represents your thinking, such as proposals, analysis, assessments or recommendations, write your own outline or rough draft before opening an AI tool.
- Use the AI as an editor and critic: "What is weak in this argument?" or "What have I missed?"
- For students, this matters doubly. Coursework exists to build skills, and an AI-written essay skips the learning it was set to produce.

## Habit 3: Ask for sources, then actually check them

Asking an AI for references is useful only if you open them. In a [2023 study in *Scientific Reports*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-41032-5), Walters and Wilder asked ChatGPT to write short literature reviews on 42 topics and checked all 636 references. They found 55% of the citations from GPT-3.5 and 18% from GPT-4 were fabricated, and that many of the real ones contained substantive errors. Newer tools, especially those that search the web, may perform differently, but the habit of checking still applies.

*Scenario (illustration):* Grace, a community health volunteer coordinator, uses an AI tool to prepare a talk on clean water. It cites a report and a statistic. She searches for the report, finds it on the publisher's site and discovers that the figure refers to a different country and an earlier year. She uses the correct figure and links to the source.

**In practice:**

- Open every link and confirm the page exists and says what the AI claims.
- Check the year the figure refers to and the place it describes.
- If you cannot find the source, do not use the claim.

## Habit 4: Know when not to use AI

Some tasks should stay entirely with people. The research above suggests that people are least able to catch AI mistakes in topics they do not know well, and that trust grows with routine use. That makes it wise to set some limits in advance.

**Consider not using a general AI assistant when:**

- The decision affects someone's health, money, legal position, job or safety, and you cannot independently check the output.
- You are dealing with a personal or sensitive matter, such as a disciplinary case, a grievance or a bereavement, where tone and care matter more than speed.
- The point of the task is for you to learn or demonstrate a skill.
- You do not know the subject well enough to judge whether the answer is right, and there is no one to check with.
- A message asks you to act urgently on money or passwords. AI also makes scams more convincing, so see our guide to [recognising AI-generated scams](/insights/recognise-ai-generated-scams).

*Scenario (illustration):* James chairs a savings group in Mbarara. A member asks whether the group should move its savings into an online investment scheme. James asks an AI tool for a quick view, then realises it cannot know the scheme's legitimacy or the members' circumstances. He tells the group the decision needs proper checks with the relevant regulator and a qualified adviser, not a chatbot.

## Habit 5: Protect confidential data

Whatever you paste into a public AI tool leaves your control. The NCSC's advice is direct: do not include sensitive information in queries to public AI models, and do not submit queries that would cause problems if they were made public.

In Uganda, there is also a legal dimension. The [Data Protection and Privacy Act, 2019](https://ulii.org/akn/ug/act/2019/9/eng@2019-05-03) applies to anyone collecting, processing, holding or using personal data in Uganda. Section 19 says that where a Uganda-based data controller or processor processes or stores personal data outside Uganda, it must ensure the other country has adequate protections at least equivalent to the Act, or that the person concerned has consented. Many AI services process data abroad, so pasting customers' or colleagues' personal details into them is not a trivial choice. Check your organisation's policy, and take proper legal advice if you handle personal data at scale.

*Scenario (illustration):* Peter, who runs a small clinic's front office, wants help drafting appointment reminder messages. Instead of pasting the patient list, he asks the AI for a template using placeholders such as [Name] and [Date], then fills in the details himself in the clinic's own system.

**In practice:**

- Remove names, phone numbers, ID numbers, account details and health information before pasting anything.
- Never paste passwords, contracts under confidentiality, or unpublished financial results into a public tool.
- Prefer tools your employer has approved and configured, and learn what their settings mean.

## A quick checklist before you hit send

1. Did I write or decide the core of this myself?
2. Have I checked every fact, figure, date and name that matters?
3. Have I opened every source the AI gave me?
4. Is this a task AI should be involved in at all?
5. Did I keep personal and confidential information out of the tool?
6. Could I explain and defend this work without the AI in the room?
