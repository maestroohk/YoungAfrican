Debates about artificial intelligence in Africa tend to start at the top: national strategies, data centres, start-up funding. Those things matter. But the question of who actually benefits from AI is being settled much lower down, in whether a student in Gulu can afford a data bundle this week, whether a market trader owns a phone that can run a modern app, and whether the tools she opens speak her language.

The argument of this article is simple. **AI will not spread its benefits across Africa faster than affordable, usable internet does.** If connectivity stays expensive and patchy, AI will mostly help people who are already connected, already skilled and already well off. If access becomes genuinely affordable, the same tools can widen opportunity instead of narrowing it.

## Most people offline are not out of range

It is natural to assume that people are offline because there is no network. For most people, that is no longer true.

The GSMA, the mobile industry association, splits the unconnected into two groups. The **coverage gap** is people who live where there is no mobile broadband network at all. The **usage gap** is people who live within range of a mobile broadband network but do not use mobile internet. Its [State of Mobile Internet Connectivity 2025 report](https://www.gsma.com/somic/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/The-State-of-Mobile-Internet-Connectivity-2025-Trends-in-Mobile-Internet-Connectivity.pdf) gives the picture for the end of 2024:

- Globally, 58% of people used mobile internet, 38% were in the usage gap and only 4% were in the coverage gap.
- In Sub-Saharan Africa, 25% of people used mobile internet, 65% were in the usage gap and 10% were in the coverage gap.
- The region's coverage gap fell from 13% in 2023 to 10% in 2024, which the GSMA equates to bringing mobile broadband coverage to 30 million people.
- Eastern Africa had the largest usage gap within the region, at 72%. Uganda appears among the 20 countries with the largest absolute usage gaps, at roughly 40 million people in 2024.

In other words, for every person in Sub-Saharan Africa without a signal in 2024, more than six had one but were not using mobile internet. Towers still matter in remote areas, but the larger problem is what happens after the signal arrives.

The International Telecommunication Union's [Facts and Figures 2025](https://www.itu.int/itu-d/reports/statistics/2025/10/15/ff25-internet-use/) points the same way. It estimates that 74% of the world's population was online in 2025, but the average for Africa was just 36%. Across income groups, the [ITU's 2025 release](https://www.itu.int/en/mediacentre/Pages/PR-2025-11-17-Facts-and-Figures.aspx) puts internet use at 94% in high-income countries and 23% in low-income countries, and 85% of urban residents compared with 58% of rural residents.

## Four barriers that also decide who benefits from AI

The GSMA notes that while coverage is a prerequisite, it is not sufficient "in the absence of affordable devices and data, digital skills and relevant content". Each of those barriers has a direct AI equivalent.

### 1. The price of data

AI tools run over the internet, so every use draws on a data bundle. Uploading a document, a photo of a diseased crop or a voice note uses data, and so does every follow-up question.

The [Alliance for Affordable Internet (A4AI)](https://adi.a4ai.org/affordable-internet-journey-from-1-to-5/) has long argued for clear price targets. Its earlier "1 for 2" benchmark held that 1GB of mobile broadband should cost no more than 2% of average monthly income. A4AI has since raised the bar, urging governments to aim for 5GB of mobile and fixed broadband at no more than 2% of average monthly income by 2026. That deadline is this year, and the ITU's [2025 affordability data](https://www.itu.int/itu-d/reports/statistics/2025/10/15/ff25-affordability-of-ict-services/) shows how far there is to go: only about four in ten low- and middle-income economies met the Broadband Commission's 2%-of-income target for at least one of its two price baskets.

### 2. The price of a device

A phone is the entry ticket. The [GSMA estimates](https://gsma.com/newsroom/press-release/gsma-calls-for-renewed-focus-on-closing-the-usage-gap-as-more-than-3-billion-people-remain-offline-despite-available-mobile-internet-services/) that in 2024 an entry-level internet-enabled handset cost 16% of average monthly income in low- and middle-income countries, rising to 48% for the poorest fifth of the population, and that this cost had barely changed since 2021. And a basic handset is only the start: apps that need more storage, memory or a better camera push the practical entry price higher.

### 3. Skills and confidence

The same GSMA release lists digital skills and literacy among the key barriers to adoption. With AI, people also need to know how to ask a useful question and how to recognise a confident but wrong answer. We cover some of that in [the AI skills worth learning in 2027](/insights/ai-skills-worth-learning-2027).

### 4. Content and language

The African Union's [Continental Artificial Intelligence Strategy](https://au.int/sites/default/files/documents/44004-doc-EN-_Continental_AI_Strategy_July_2024.pdf) (July 2024) is blunt about this. It names gaps in internet usage, a lack of computing platforms, limited training data and scarce AI skills as the main inhibitors of AI uptake. It notes that internet use is held back by affordability, limited skills and "a lack of content that appeals to users", and that a lack of data is a barrier to building tools that understand speech or text in African languages.

That last point creates a loop. Tools that work poorly in Luganda, Runyankore or Acholi give people fewer reasons to go online; fewer people online means less locally produced text and speech; and less local data means the tools stay weak in those languages.

## Compute and data: the less visible divide

Affordable access is not only about end users. Anyone trying to build AI for African problems, whether a university lab, a health researcher or a small software firm, needs bandwidth, storage and computing power.

The AU strategy records that Africa, with 15% of the global population, accounted for only 1.8% of the world's large-scale data centres in 2023, and it estimates that only about 10% of data centre demand in Africa is currently served. It also observes that while research and education networks can connect institutions to international computing resources, a lack of adequate bandwidth makes it hard to use them for experimentation.

The strategy warns that AI applications developed in other regions "are likely to lack contextual relevance". Cheaper, more reliable bandwidth is part of how African developers and researchers get to build their own.

## Shared access: useful, but not the whole answer

If the usage gap is mainly about cost, then models that spread cost across many people deserve serious attention. Two are especially relevant.

**Community networks.** The Internet Society describes [community networks](https://www.internetsociety.org/news/press-releases/2018/community-networks-key-to-connecting-africa-says-internet-society/) as "communications infrastructure built, managed and used by local communities". In these models, a community or local organisation runs the network and keeps decisions, and often revenue, close to home.

**Community Wi-Fi hotspots.** A hotspot brings one good internet connection to a shared place, such as a trading centre, school, health centre or church compound, and lets many people use it through low-cost vouchers sold by the hour, the day or the amount of data. Because a single backhaul link serves many users, the cost per person can be lower than everyone buying individual mobile bundles, and people can buy access in small amounts that match irregular incomes. We explain the mechanics in [how community Wi-Fi works](/insights/how-community-wifi-works).

These models have real limitations, and it is worth being honest about them:

- **They are local.** A hotspot serves people within range of it. It does not help a farmer checking prices from the field or a nurse on a home visit.
- **They do not solve the device problem.** Shared Wi-Fi lowers the cost of data, not the cost of a phone or laptop.
- **They depend on power and upkeep.** Reliable electricity, security for equipment and someone accountable for repairs all matter as much as the hardware.
- **Shared access is not equal access.** The GSMA found that in 2024 some 710 million people used mobile internet on a device they did not own, and it warns that people with only "temporary, shared or intermittent access" are unable to realise the full benefits of being online.
- **Quality varies.** A congested hotspot at peak times struggles with data-heavy tasks such as video lessons.

So shared access is best understood as a bridge, not a destination. It can get students, traders and community groups online affordably today and build skills and demand, while longer-term work continues on device costs, taxes and local content. The GSMA's recommendations for Sub-Saharan Africa include removing sector-specific taxes on mobile services and handsets.

## The test that matters

Africa's AI future will be judged by a plain question: can an ordinary person, on an ordinary income, use these tools when they need them? Answering it means treating affordable data, devices, skills and local-language content as AI policy, not as a separate, older problem.
