If you are a student or early in your career, you have probably been told to "learn AI" without anyone explaining what that means. Does it mean coding? Buying a course? Using a chatbot every day? This guide takes a narrower view. It sets out six skills that the evidence suggests employers value, how to start each one this month on a modest budget, and what you can stop worrying about.

## What the evidence actually says

The World Economic Forum's [Future of Jobs Report 2025](https://reports.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Future_of_Jobs_Report_2025.pdf), based on a survey of more than 1,000 employers, found that workers can expect 39% of their existing skill sets to be transformed or become outdated between 2025 and 2030. It lists AI and big data as the fastest-growing skills, followed by networks and cybersecurity and technological literacy. Yet the most sought-after core skill was analytical thinking, which seven in ten companies considered essential.

That combination matters. Employers want people who can use the tools *and* think clearly about what the tools produce.

In a 2024 survey of 31,000 people across 31 countries, Microsoft and LinkedIn found that 66% of leaders said they would not hire someone without AI skills, while only 39% of AI users had received AI training from their company ([Microsoft](https://news.microsoft.com/source/2024/05/08/microsoft-and-linkedin-release-the-2024-work-trend-index-on-the-state-of-ai-at-work/)). In other words, most people are teaching themselves.

The International Labour Organization estimates that one in four workers globally is in an occupation with some exposure to generative AI, with clerical work the most exposed. It also finds exposure is much lower in low-income countries (11% of employment) than in high-income countries (34%), and that transformation of jobs, not wholesale replacement, is the most likely impact ([ILO](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-refined-global-index-occupational-exposure)). For Ugandans, that suggests time to prepare rather than reason to panic.

## Skill 1: Asking clearly for what you want

Getting useful output from an AI assistant is mostly about giving clear instructions. Google's prompting guidance breaks a good request into four parts: persona, task, context and format ([Google Workspace](https://workspace.google.com/resources/ai/writing-effective-prompts/)). For example: "You are a careers adviser (persona). Suggest five questions I should ask at an internship interview (task). I am a second-year accounting student in Kampala applying to a small audit firm (context). Give them as a numbered list with one line explaining each (format)."

**How to start:** Pick one real task each day this week, such as summarising lecture notes or drafting an email, and write the request with all four parts. Compare the result with a lazy one-line request.

**Skip:** Paid "prompt engineering" certificates and lists of "100 magic prompts". The skill is clear writing, and you build it by practising on your own work.

## Skill 2: Checking what the AI tells you

This is the most valuable skill on the list and the one most people neglect. AI tools can produce what the US National Institute of Standards and Technology calls confabulation: "confidently stated but erroneous or false content" ([NIST AI 600-1](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf)). That includes invented statistics and references that look real but do not exist.

**How to start:**

1. When an AI gives you a fact, ask yourself where it would have come from, then find that original source yourself.
2. If it cites a report or article, open it. If you cannot find it, assume it is invented.
3. Check any calculation in a spreadsheet or on a calculator.
4. For anything about law, tax, health or money, go to the official body or a qualified person.

We go deeper on this habit in our piece on [critical thinking when using AI at work](/insights/ai-at-work-critical-thinking).

**Skip:** "AI detector" tools that claim to tell you whether text was machine-written. Your judgement about whether something is *true* matters far more than guessing who wrote it.

## Skill 3: Working with data in a spreadsheet

"AI and big data" sounds intimidating, but for most jobs the entry point is a spreadsheet. If you can clean a messy list, use basic formulas, sort and filter, and make a simple chart, you can work with AI tools on real data and check their answers.

**How to start:** Take something you already track, such as your monthly spending or a club's membership list, and put it in Google Sheets or Excel. Ask an AI assistant to explain one formula at a time (SUM, AVERAGE, COUNTIF, then a lookup function), and test each on your own data. Remove names and phone numbers before sharing any real data with an AI tool.

**Skip:** Jumping straight into machine learning courses before you are comfortable with spreadsheets. The foundations come first.

## Skill 4: Privacy and security habits

Networks and cybersecurity was the second fastest-growing skill area in the WEF report. You do not need to become a security specialist, but you do need sensible habits.

Know what happens to what you type. Google, for instance, says a subset of Gemini chats are reviewed by humans and advises users not to enter confidential information they would not want a reviewer to see ([Google Gemini Help](https://support.google.com/gemini/answer/13594961)). If you work with other people's information, learn the basics of Uganda's Data Protection and Privacy Act: consent before collecting personal data, keeping it secure, limits on sending it abroad, and registration with the Personal Data Protection Office ([DLA Piper summary](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/index.html?t=law&c=UG)).

**How to start:** Check the privacy and history settings of each AI tool you use. Turn on two-step verification for your email and mobile money. Learn to [recognise AI-generated scams](/insights/recognise-ai-generated-scams), because fraudsters use these tools too.

**Skip:** Any course that promises to make you a "hacker" in a weekend.

## Skill 5: Communicating across languages

Uganda is multilingual, and AI translation is improving. Google added Luganda to Google Translate in 2022, while noting the method behind its new languages "isn't perfect" ([Google](https://blog.google/products/translate/24-new-languages/)). People who can use these tools for a first draft and then fix the result properly are useful in customer service, health outreach, agriculture and media.

**How to start:** Translate a short paragraph you know well into a language you speak fluently, then list every error. That exercise teaches you exactly where the tool can and cannot be trusted.

**Skip:** Relying on machine translation for anything legal, medical or financial without a fluent human check.

## Skill 6: Understanding how AI works, without the maths

You do not need to code to understand what these systems are, why they make mistakes and where they are unfair. UNESCO's [AI competency framework for students](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/ai-competency-framework-students) sets out 12 competencies across four dimensions, including a human-centred mindset and the ethics of AI, not just technical skill.

**How to start:** The free *Elements of AI* course from the University of Helsinki and MinnaLearn has an introduction that requires "no complicated math or programming" ([Elements of AI](https://www.elementsofai.com/)). Work through it slowly, a chapter a week. Download or read pages while on Wi-Fi to save mobile data.

**Skip:** Building your own large AI model from scratch, unless you are deliberately heading towards a technical AI career. Even then, learn programming and statistics first.

## The human skills underneath

The WEF report also lists creative thinking, resilience, flexibility and agility, and curiosity and lifelong learning as rising in importance. Those sound soft, but they are what let you keep learning as tools change. The specific apps you use in 2027 may not exist in 2030. The habit of learning a new tool carefully, checking its work and explaining your reasoning will.

## What not to waste money on

- **"Get rich with AI" courses.** Anyone selling a system that earns guaranteed income is selling the course, not the income.
- **AI trading bots and price predictions.** No tool can reliably predict markets. Read [can AI predict crypto prices?](/insights/can-ai-predict-crypto-prices) before you spend anything.
- **Chasing every new tool.** Learn one general assistant and one spreadsheet program well.
- **Premium subscriptions too early.** Free tiers are enough to build every skill on this list. Upgrade only when a specific limit is blocking real work.

## A realistic three-month plan

1. **Month one:** Practise clear requests (Skill 1) and fact-checking (Skill 2) on your studies or job every day.
2. **Month two:** Build one spreadsheet project from real data (Skill 3) and review your privacy settings (Skill 4).
3. **Month three:** Work through the free *Elements of AI* introduction (Skill 6) and try a translation check (Skill 5).

Keep a short note of what you tried and what went wrong. That record is more convincing to an employer than any certificate, because it shows how you think.
