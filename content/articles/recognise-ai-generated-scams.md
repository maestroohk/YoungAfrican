Scams have always relied on the same few levers: a sense of urgency, a trusted name, and a request for money or a secret. What generative AI changes is the packaging. A message can now arrive in flawless, friendly English, a voice can sound like your brother, and a video call can show a face you recognise.

The US Federal Bureau of Investigation warned in December 2024 that criminals are using generative AI to "commit fraud on a larger scale" and to make their schemes more believable, across text, images, cloned audio and video ([FBI IC3](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2024/PSA241203)). The good news is that the defences have not changed much. They just need to become habits.

Use this guide in two ways: read the scam types to know what to expect, then keep the checklist at the end somewhere you will see it.

## The one rule that covers almost everything

**Verify through a channel you already trust, not the one the request arrived on.** If a call, message or video asks you to move money or share a code, end the conversation and make contact yourself, using a number or account you already had before the request came in. Almost every scam below fails at this step.

## Scam type 1: the cloned voice

**What it looks like.** A call or WhatsApp voice note from someone who sounds exactly like a relative, a boss or a friend. They are in trouble: an accident, an arrest, a hospital bill, a phone that "just broke". They need money now, often by mobile money, and they ask you not to tell anyone.

**Why it works.** The US Federal Trade Commission explains that a scammer needs only a short clip of someone's voice, which may be found online, and a voice-cloning program ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2023/03/scammers-use-ai-enhance-their-family-emergency-schemes)). Voice notes and videos shared publicly can be enough raw material.

**Warning signs:**

- An emergency that requires money immediately and secrecy.
- A new number, or a familiar contact "calling from a friend's phone".
- Payment by methods that are hard to reverse.
- Pressure that rises when you ask questions.

**What to do.** The FTC's advice is simple: "Don't trust the voice." Hang up and call the person on a number you know is theirs. If you cannot reach them, contact another family member or friend who would know.

## Scam type 2: the deepfake video call

**What it looks like.** A video call where the person on screen appears to be a senior colleague, a public figure or someone you have been chatting with. The FBI notes criminals use AI-generated video for real-time calls with supposed executives or authority figures, and to "prove" that an online contact is a real person ([FBI IC3](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2024/PSA241203)).

**A real case.** In 2024 the engineering firm Arup confirmed to CNN that "fake voices and images were used" in a fraud in which a finance worker in Hong Kong attended a video call with people he believed were the company's chief financial officer and other staff. According to Hong Kong police, the worker sent about 200 million Hong Kong dollars, roughly US$25.6 million. He had initially suspected the request was a phishing email ([CNN, via ABC17](https://abc17news.com/money/cnn-business-consumer/2024/05/16/british-engineering-giant-arup-revealed-as-25-million-deepfake-scam-victim/)). Seeing familiar faces overcame a sensible first instinct.

**Warning signs.** The FBI suggests looking for visual flaws such as distorted hands, odd eyes or teeth, unnatural shadows, lag and stiff movement, and listening for an unusual tone or choice of words. These clues are useful, but do not rely on spotting them; the technology is improving. Rely on the procedure instead: a video call is never, on its own, enough reason to send money.

## Scam type 3: phishing that reads perfectly

**What it looks like.** An SMS, email or WhatsApp message claiming to be from your bank, mobile network, employer, a delivery company or a government office. It asks you to click a link, "confirm" details or read back a code you have just received.

**Why it works.** Spelling mistakes used to give scams away. The FBI notes that criminals now use AI tools and translation to remove grammar and spelling errors ([FBI IC3](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2024/PSA241203)). Judge the message by what it asks for, not how well it is written. The UK's National Cyber Security Centre suggests checking for appeals to **authority**, **urgency**, **emotion** and **scarcity**, and asking whether you were actually expecting such a message ([NCSC](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/collection/phishing-scams/spot-scams)).

**Codes are the target.** Many phishing attempts are really after a one-time code or PIN.

- MTN Uganda states that "under no circumstances" should a customer share their PIN, code or one-time password with anyone, and says it calls customers only from 031 212 0000 ([MTN Uganda](https://www.mtn.co.ug/?p=116954)). If in doubt, check the current number on MTN's official website or with customer care.
- WhatsApp says you should never share your registration code "not even friends or family" ([WhatsApp Help Center](https://faq.whatsapp.com/1131652977717250)). Someone who gets that code can take over your account and message your contacts as you.

## Scam type 4: the fake investment platform

**What it looks like.** An advert, a "mentor" on Telegram or WhatsApp, or a polished website offering an AI trading bot, a crypto arbitrage system or a forex signal group. A dashboard shows your balance rising fast. Withdrawals become difficult once you have deposited more.

**What regulators say.** The FBI warns that criminals build fraudulent cryptocurrency investment websites using AI-generated content, including chatbots ([FBI IC3](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2024/PSA241203)). The FTC is blunt: "Only scammers will guarantee profits or big returns," and "only scammers demand payment in cryptocurrency" ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-cryptocurrency-and-scams)). Celebrity endorsement videos can be faked too, and a joint alert from the SEC, FINRA and NASAA warns that deepfake videos may imitate company leaders announcing false news ([FINRA](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/artificial-intelligence-and-investment-fraud)).

**Check the regulator, not the website.** In Uganda, the Capital Markets Authority publishes a list of approved investment advisers and service providers on its website and urges the public to deal only with licensed professionals ([CMA Uganda](https://cmauganda.co.ug/?p=3178)). For a firm claiming to be UK-based, the Financial Conduct Authority keeps a Warning List of unauthorised firms and a Firm Checker; it advises using the Firm Checker's contact details rather than any the firm gives you ([FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/scamsmart)). A firm missing from a warning list is not proof it is safe.

## Scam type 5: romance and long-game impersonation

**What it looks like.** A warm, attentive contact met on social media, a dating app or a wrong-number message. Their photos may be AI-generated. Over weeks they build trust, then a crisis appears, or they offer to teach you how they "make money with crypto".

**Warning signs from the FTC** ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-romance-scams)):

- They quickly want to move the conversation off the platform.
- They live far away and always have a reason they cannot meet.
- They ask for money, or offer to help you start investing in cryptocurrency.

**What to do.** Talk to someone you trust before sending anything. The FTC also suggests a reverse image search of their profile picture. Its core rule: "Never send money or gifts to a sweetheart you haven't met in person."

## Secrets that no genuine person will ask for

Some information should never leave you, whoever asks and however convincing they sound:

- **Mobile money and bank PINs**, and one-time passwords.
- **WhatsApp registration codes.**
- **Crypto wallet recovery (seed) phrases.** The wallet maker Ledger warns that "anyone that has your recovery phrase has full access" to the accounts created with it, and that its support staff will never ask for it ([Ledger](https://www.ledger.com/ongoing-phishing-campaigns)).

If someone asks for one of these, you have identified a scam. No further analysis is needed.

## Your verification checklist

1. **Pause.** Urgency is the scammer's main tool. A genuine emergency can survive a five-minute check.
2. **Call back on a number you already have**, not one given in the message. The FBI recommends hanging up and contacting the organisation through a number you find yourself.
3. **Agree a family code word.** Both the FBI and the SEC, FINRA and NASAA alert suggest a secret word or phrase that family members can use to confirm who they are in an emergency. Choose something that is not posted online.
4. **Never share PINs, codes or seed phrases.** See the list above.
5. **Check the regulator's list**, such as the CMA in Uganda, before sending money to any investment scheme.
6. **Limit the raw material.** The FBI suggests limiting public images and voice recordings and keeping social accounts private.
7. **Tell someone.** Scams thrive on secrecy. Talking it through with a friend often breaks the spell.
8. **Act quickly if you have paid.** Contact your bank or mobile money provider immediately and report the incident to the police.
